Essential mineral

Sodium, the salt mineral

Sodium is an essential mineral that helps control blood volume and blood pressure and keeps muscles and nerves working, but too much is linked with high blood pressure1.

MineralsTracked in mgDaily limit10 sources · checked 2026-09-27

How much you need

01 What it does

What sodium does for you.

Blood volume & pressure

Your body uses sodium to control blood pressure and blood volume3.

Nerve signals

Needed for the transmission of nerve impulses2.

Muscle function

Your muscles need a small amount of sodium to work properly1.

Acid-base balance

Helps maintain plasma volume, acid-base balance and normal cell function2.

02 How your body uses it

How your body handles sodium.

About 90% of the sodium you eat is absorbed in the small intestine and colon, and about half of the body's sodium sits in the fluid outside cells4. The kidneys filter it and, guided by hormones such as angiotensin II and aldosterone, reabsorb what the body needs, while small amounts are lost in sweat and stool4.

Hidden sodium additives

Sodium is also added as MSG, sodium nitrite, baking soda and sodium benzoate3.

Potassium salt swaps

WHO lists lower-sodium salt substitutes that contain potassium as one way to reduce sodium intake2.

04 How much you need

Your daily reference amounts.

The same reference table draws the reference lines on your charts in Ayuvo. Needs differ in pregnancy and breastfeeding, which are not covered here.

2,300 mgReference limit per day for adults: Chronic Disease Risk Reduction Intake (CDRR)910
  • 2,300 mg is the Chronic Disease Risk Reduction Intake (CDRR): cutting intake above it is expected to lower chronic disease risk.
  • The Adequate Intake (AI) for adults is 1,500 mg.
05 Balance

Too little, too much.

Signs like these have many possible causes. They are a reason to talk to a clinician, not to self-treat.

Too little

Low blood sodium (hyponatremia) is usually linked to medicines such as diuretics, fluid losses, or heart, kidney or liver conditions6.

  • Confusion or restlessness
  • Headache
  • Fatigue
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Muscle weakness or cramps
  • Seizures in severe cases

Too much

Eating too much sodium is linked with high blood pressure, a major cause of heart disease and stroke1.

  • Raised blood pressure
  • Higher heart disease and stroke risk
  • Kidney disease risk
  • Fluid buildup in heart failure
  • Stomach cancer risk
  • Osteoporosis risk
06 Blood test

Blood sodium test.

A blood test measures the concentration of sodium in your blood; high or low values can reflect fluid losses, hormone or kidney problems, or medicines5.

Blood sodiumWhat it may mean
135–145 mEq/LNormal range
Below 135 mEq/LLow (hyponatremia)
Above 145 mEq/LHigh (hypernatremia)

Labs use different units and ranges. Your clinician reads a result alongside your history and other tests.

07 Key points

Remember these five.

  1. Most sodium comes from packaged and restaurant foods, not the salt shaker.
  2. Compare Nutrition Facts labels and pick the lower-sodium version of the same food.
  3. Cooking at home and seasoning with herbs and spices makes it easier to use less salt.
  4. Rinsing canned foods such as beans and vegetables can remove some sodium.
  5. Cutting back when intake is above the level on this page is expected to lower chronic disease risk.
FAQ

Questions about sodium.

Is sodium the same as salt?

Not quite. Table salt is one source of sodium, but sodium is also found naturally in foods like milk and meat and is added as ingredients such as MSG, baking soda and sodium nitrite. Food labels list sodium, not salt.

Where does most of the sodium I eat come from?

Over 70% comes from packaged and prepared foods rather than salt added while cooking or eating. Deli meat sandwiches, pizza, savory snacks, soups, bread and cheese are among the largest contributors in the U.S.

What does a blood sodium test show?

It measures how concentrated sodium is in your blood, with a normal range of about 135 to 145 mEq/L. Low or high values usually point to fluid losses, medicines such as diuretics, or hormone, kidney, heart or liver conditions, and are interpreted by a clinician.
