Essential mineral

Phosphorus, for bones and energy

Phosphorus is a mineral found in bones, teeth, DNA and ATP, the molecule cells use to store energy, and many protein-rich foods provide it1.

MineralsTracked in mgDaily target3 sources · checked 2026-09-27

How much you need

01 What it does

What phosphorus does for you.

Bones & teeth

With calcium it forms hydroxyapatite, the main building block of bone and tooth enamel; about 85% of body phosphorus is there1.

Energy (ATP)

Part of ATP, the body's key energy source, and involved in storing energy inside cells1.

DNA & membranes

A component of DNA and RNA and, as phospholipids, of cell membranes1.

pH balance

Helps maintain normal pH in the fluid outside cells and helps activate enzymes1.

Muscles, heart & nerves

Works with B vitamins in kidney function, muscle contractions, a normal heartbeat and nerve signalling2.

02 How your body uses it

How your body uses phosphorus.

Phosphorus is absorbed in the small intestine, mostly passively and partly by active transport, and the kidneys, bones and intestines keep blood levels in balance1. Calcitriol (active vitamin D) increases uptake from the diet, while parathyroid hormone and FGF23, made mainly by bone cells, make the kidneys excrete more phosphate3.

Phosphorus in seeds, beans and unleavened bread is mostly phytic acid, which the body absorbs poorly; overall 40–70% of food phosphorus is absorbed, more from animal foods than plants1. Amounts per serving from1.

Phosphate additives

Additives such as phosphoric acid and sodium phosphate in processed foods can add a sizeable share of daily phosphorus, and about 70% of it is absorbed1.

Supplements

Sold alone, in combinations and in a few multivitamins; most products provide only a small amount1.

Food labels

U.S. labels have to list phosphorus only when it has been added to the food1.

05 Balance

Too little, too much.

Signs like these have many possible causes. They are a reason to talk to a clinician, not to self-treat.

Too little

Deficiency (hypophosphatemia) is rare and almost never caused by a low intake; it usually comes from medical conditions, and long-term use of aluminium antacids can contribute1.

  • Loss of appetite
  • Anemia
  • Muscle weakness
  • Bone pain, rickets or osteomalacia
  • More infections
  • Tingling, poor coordination, confusion

Too much

High intakes rarely cause problems in healthy people, but very large short-term doses, such as sodium phosphate laxatives above the label dose, can push blood phosphate too high1.

  • High blood phosphate (hyperphosphatemia)
  • Changes in calcium-regulating hormones
  • Calcium deposits in soft tissues
  • Build-up when kidney function is low
  • Higher blood phosphate linked with heart disease
06 Blood test

Serum phosphate.

Phosphorus status is not usually assessed, but phosphate can be measured in serum or plasma; blood levels do not necessarily reflect how much phosphorus the whole body holds1.

07 Supplements

Supplement forms.

Phosphorus comes in phosphorus-only products, combination supplements and a few multivitamins, usually as phosphate salts or phospholipids1.

Phosphate salts

Such as dipotassium or disodium phosphate; about 70% is absorbed1.

Phospholipids

Such as phosphatidylcholine or phosphatidylserine; how well their phosphorus is absorbed has not been measured in people1.

Sodium phosphate laxatives

Not a supplement, but a large phosphorus source; doses above the label have been linked with serious harm, especially with kidney or heart disease1.

Supplements can interact with medicines. Talk to a clinician or pharmacist before you start one, especially at high doses.

08 Key points

Remember these five.

  1. Phosphorus is in most protein-rich foods, from dairy and meat to beans, lentils and nuts.
  2. About 85% of the body's phosphorus sits in bones and teeth, bound up with calcium.
  3. Phosphate additives in processed foods add to intake and are absorbed well.
  4. Phosphorus in beans, seeds and whole grains is partly locked in phytic acid and absorbed less.
  5. When kidney function declines, phosphate is cleared less well and blood levels can rise.
FAQ

Questions about phosphorus.

Do most people get enough phosphorus?

Yes. Surveys show most U.S. adults eat more than the recommended amount, and deficiency is almost never caused by diet. When it happens, it is usually linked with medical conditions, some medicines or severe malnutrition.

Why does phosphorus matter in kidney disease?

Healthy kidneys remove extra phosphate in urine. When kidney function declines, phosphate can build up in the blood, which is linked with bone problems and calcium deposits in blood vessels. People with kidney disease usually get specific advice on phosphorus from their care team.

Does cola contain a lot of phosphorus?

Cola contains phosphoric acid, but a cup provides only about 18 mg of phosphorus, far less than a cup of milk. Phosphate additives spread across many processed foods add more to total intake than any single drink.
