Trace mineral

Iodine, for your thyroid

Iodine is a trace mineral your thyroid needs to make the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which regulate metabolism and are required for normal growth and brain development1.

How much you need

01 What it does

What iodine does for you.

Thyroid hormones

An essential part of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), the thyroid hormones1.

Metabolism

Thyroid hormones regulate protein synthesis and enzyme activity and help set metabolic activity1.

Growth & development

Needed for normal bone and brain development in fetuses and infants1.

Steady thyroid

With enough iodine, TSH keeps hormone levels steady; without it the thyroid can enlarge into a goiter1.

Immune response

Iodine appears to play a role in immune response as well1.

02 How your body uses it

How your body uses iodine.

Iodide is quickly and almost completely absorbed in the stomach and duodenum; the thyroid concentrates what it needs and most of the rest leaves in urine1. In thyroid cells the sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) brings iodide in, it is bound to tyrosines on thyroglobulin, and the coupled hormones T4 and T3 are released into the blood3. TSH from the pituitary raises thyroid iodine uptake and hormone release1.

Iodine in food varies widely: milk depends on cow feed and cleaning agents, only bread made with iodate dough conditioners is rich in it, and seaweeds range enormously1. Amounts per serving from1.

Iodized salt

Many countries iodize table salt, but sea, kosher and Himalayan salts usually are not, and processed foods rarely use iodized salt1.

Supplements

Many multivitamin/mineral products contain iodine; iodine-only products are often high dose1.

Soil and region

Crops from iodine-poor soils, such as mountain areas and flood-prone river valleys, hold less iodine1.

05 Balance

Too little, too much.

Signs like these have many possible causes. They are a reason to talk to a clinician, not to self-treat.

Too little

Low iodine limits thyroid hormone production; goiter is usually the earliest sign, and deficiency in pregnancy can cause lasting harm to the baby1.

  • Goiter (enlarged thyroid)
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Impaired mental function in adults
  • Lower IQ in children
  • Harm to fetal development in pregnancy

Too much

High intakes can cause some of the same problems as too little, because excess iodine can block thyroid hormone synthesis in susceptible people1.

  • Goiter
  • Raised TSH and hypothyroidism
  • Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism
  • Thyroid inflammation (thyroiditis)
  • Burning mouth and stomach at very large doses
  • Nausea, vomiting and diarrhea
06 Urine test

Urinary iodine (population level).

More than 90% of dietary iodine leaves in urine, so the median urinary iodine of a group reflects its recent intake1. One spot sample varies from day to day, so the WHO cutoffs below describe populations, not individuals; they are based on school-age children and also apply to non-pregnant adults2.

07 Supplements

Supplement forms.

Iodine in supplements is usually potassium iodide or sodium iodide, and some products use kelp1.

Potassium iodide

Very well absorbed; one small study found about 96% absorption1.

Sodium iodide

Another common form in multivitamin/mineral products1.

Kelp

A seaweed-based source; iodine content varies greatly between seaweeds1.

Iodine-only products

Many contain high doses, sometimes above the upper limit on this page1.

Supplements can interact with medicines. Talk to a clinician or pharmacist before you start one, especially at high doses.

08 Key points

Remember these five.

  1. Iodine is needed to make the thyroid hormones T4 and T3.
  2. Seafood, seaweed, dairy, eggs and iodized salt are the main sources; most fruit and vegetables have little.
  3. Sea, kosher and Himalayan salts are usually not iodized, and processed foods rarely use iodized salt.
  4. Both too little and too much iodine can disturb thyroid function.
  5. Urinary iodine describes groups of people; one spot sample says little about one person.
FAQ

Questions about iodine.

Is sea salt a good source of iodine?

Usually not. Specialty salts such as sea salt, kosher salt, Himalayan salt and fleur de sel are not usually iodized, and noniodized sea salt provides almost no iodine. The label says when a salt is iodized.

Do vegans get enough iodine?

Seafood, eggs, milk and milk products are among the best sources, so vegans and people who eat few of these foods might not get enough unless they use iodized salt or other iodine sources. Plant milks and most fruit and vegetables contain little iodine.

Do cabbage, broccoli or soy harm the thyroid?

These foods contain goitrogens, which can interfere with iodine uptake by the thyroid. This mainly matters for people who already get too little iodine. For most people who eat a varied diet with enough iodine, reasonable amounts are not a concern.
