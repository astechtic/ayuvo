Water-soluble B vitamin

Vitamin B6, the enzyme helper

Vitamin B6 is a water-soluble vitamin whose coenzyme forms take part in more than 100 enzyme reactions, mostly in protein metabolism, and support brain, blood and immune function1.

How much you need

01 What it does

What vitamin B6 does for you.

Protein metabolism

Its coenzyme forms, PLP and PMP, run more than 100 enzyme reactions, mostly in amino acid and protein metabolism1.

Brain messengers

Needed to make neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, which plays a role in cognitive development13.

Immune function

Involved in immune function, for example by promoting lymphocyte and interleukin-2 production1.

Hemoglobin

Involved in forming hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen1.

Glucose supply

Takes part in releasing glucose from glycogen and in making new glucose (gluconeogenesis)1.

02 How your body uses it

How your body uses vitamin B6.

Phosphorylated food forms are split and free vitamin B6 is absorbed by passive diffusion in the jejunum1. Pyridoxal kinase turns it into PLP, which travels in plasma bound to albumin; much of the body's PLP sits in muscle bound to glycogen phosphorylase, and 4-pyridoxic acid is the main form in urine2. PLP is dephosphorylated before it enters cells3.

Fortified cereals

In the U.S., adults get most of their vitamin B6 from fortified cereals, beef, poultry, starchy vegetables and some noncitrus fruits1.

Supplements

Found in multivitamins, B-complex products and B6-only supplements; about 28–36% of people use a supplement that contains it1.

Stored in muscle

Much of the body's PLP is held in muscle, bound to the enzyme glycogen phosphorylase2.

05 Balance

Too little, too much.

Signs like these have many possible causes. They are a reason to talk to a clinician, not to self-treat.

Too little

Isolated vitamin B6 deficiency is uncommon; low B6 usually comes with low levels of other B vitamins such as B12 and folic acid1.

  • Microcytic anemia
  • Scaly lips and cracks at the mouth corners
  • Swollen tongue (glossitis)
  • Depression and confusion
  • Weakened immune function

Too much

Vitamin B6 from food has not been linked with harm, but long-term high-dose pyridoxine supplements can cause sensory nerve damage; nerve damage is also the basis of the 2023 European upper limit14.

  • Sensory nerve damage (neuropathy)
  • Loss of control of body movements (ataxia)
  • Painful skin lesions
  • Sensitivity to sunlight
  • Nausea and heartburn
06 Blood test

Plasma pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP).

Plasma PLP is the most common measure of vitamin B6 status; B6 can also be measured in red blood cells or urine, or indirectly through enzyme and tryptophan tests1.

Plasma PLPWhat it may mean
Above 30 nmol/LTraditional sign of adequate status in adults
Below 20 nmol/LLow; 20 nmol/L is the adequacy cutoff used to set the recommended amounts

Labs use different units and ranges. Your clinician reads a result alongside your history and other tests.

07 Supplements

Supplement forms.

Supplements most often contain pyridoxine hydrochloride, and absorption from supplements is similar to food and does not differ much between forms1.

Pyridoxine hydrochloride

The most common B6 form in supplements; the body absorbs large doses well but quickly removes most of it in the urine1.

Pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP)

The active coenzyme form, used in some supplements1.

Tablets, capsules and liquids

Sold as capsules, tablets (including sublingual and chewable ones) and liquids1.

Supplements can interact with medicines. Talk to a clinician or pharmacist before you start one, especially at high doses.

08 Key points

Remember these five.

  1. Vitamin B6 is a group of six related compounds; PLP is the main active form.
  2. Chickpeas, fish, poultry, potatoes and noncitrus fruit such as bananas are good sources.
  3. Low vitamin B6 usually shows up alongside low levels of other B vitamins.
  4. Plasma PLP is the most common measure of vitamin B6 status.
  5. Long-term high-dose supplements, not food, are linked with nerve damage.
FAQ

Questions about vitamin B6.

Can you get too much vitamin B6 from supplements?

Vitamin B6 from food has not been reported to cause problems. Taking high doses of pyridoxine for months or years can damage sensory nerves and affect control of body movements, and symptoms usually stop if the supplement is stopped early. In 2023 the European Food Safety Authority set a lower upper limit than the U.S. one, based on nerve damage.

Does vitamin B6 lower heart disease risk?

Vitamin B6, folic acid and vitamin B12 can lower homocysteine, but most large trials found that B vitamin supplements did not reduce heart attacks or strokes. So far there is little evidence that vitamin B6 supplements lower cardiovascular risk.

Who is more likely to have low vitamin B6?

Low vitamin B6 is more common in people with kidney disease, autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or celiac disease, and alcohol dependence. Some antiepileptic medicines can also lower it over time.
