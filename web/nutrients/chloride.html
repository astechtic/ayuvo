Electrolyte

Chloride, salt's other half

Chloride is an electrolyte, eaten mostly as salt (sodium chloride), that helps keep body fluids in balance and is an essential part of stomach juices2.

MineralsTracked in mgDaily target4 sources · checked 2026-09-27

How much you need

01 What it does

What chloride does for you.

Fluid balance

Helps keep osmotic balance and the movement of water and solutes between body fluids1.

Stomach acid

Needed to make hydrochloric acid in the stomach, which helps digest protein3.

Acid-base balance

The main negative ion outside cells; it keeps body fluids electrically neutral and helps hold pH steady3.

Carbon dioxide transport

In red blood cells, swapping chloride for bicarbonate helps carry carbon dioxide to the lungs1.

Muscles & nerves

Contributes to normal muscle and nerve activity1.

02 How your body uses it

How your body uses chloride.

Chloride is absorbed efficiently along the small and large intestine, often coupled to sodium, and most of it stays in the fluid outside cells1. The kidneys filter large amounts and reabsorb more than 99%, so urine is the main route out; smaller amounts leave in sweat and stool1.

Processed foods

Salt added during industrial processing and preservation is the main source of chloride in Western diets1.

Salt substitutes

Potassium chloride, a common salt substitute, also provides chloride2.

05 Balance

Too little, too much.

Signs like these have many possible causes. They are a reason to talk to a clinician, not to self-treat.

Too little

Dietary chloride deficiency is rare1; low levels usually follow large fluid losses from heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhea, or some diuretic medicines2.

  • Weakness
  • Lethargy and irritability
  • Loss of appetite
  • Digestive symptoms
  • Metabolic alkalosis
  • Poor growth in infants

Too much

Chloride is eaten mostly as sodium chloride, and too much salt is linked with higher blood pressure and fluid build-up in some conditions2.

  • Higher blood pressure
  • Fluid build-up in heart failure
  • Fluid build-up in cirrhosis or kidney disease
  • Salt raises blood pressure more than other sodium forms
06 Key points

Remember these five.

  1. Most chloride comes from salt: each gram of salt carries about 0.6 g of chloride.
  2. Unprocessed foods contain chloride, but only in small amounts.
  3. Chloride keeps body fluids and pH in balance and is part of stomach acid.
  4. Deficiency from diet is rare; heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhea are more common causes of low levels.
  5. Eating less salt lowers sodium and chloride intake together.
FAQ

Questions about chloride.

Is chloride the same as chlorine?

No. Chlorine is a reactive gas used, for example, to disinfect water. Chloride is the charged form of the element found in salt and in body fluids, and it is the form the body uses.

Can a blood test show how much chloride I eat?

Not really. Blood chloride is often measured as part of an electrolyte panel, but the body keeps it in a tight range, so it says little about intake. Unusual results usually reflect fluid losses, kidney function or acid-base changes rather than diet.

Do salt substitutes contain chloride?

Yes. Many salt substitutes use potassium chloride, so they still provide chloride while replacing some of the sodium with potassium.
